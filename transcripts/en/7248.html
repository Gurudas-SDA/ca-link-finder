||9||Regarding 'of a person,' etc. Here this is said: Although ten means of knowledge are known—namely, perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), authority (ārṣa), comparison (upamāṇa), postulation (arthāpatti), non-perception (abhāva), inclusion (sambhava), tradition (aitihya), and gesture (ceṣṭā)—nevertheless, testimony (śabda), which is speech free from the defects of illusion (bhrama), inattention (pramāda), the desire to deceive (vipralipsā), and sensory inadequacy (karaṇāpāṭava), is the root means of knowledge. This is because the others are generally tainted by human defects like illusion, leading to erroneous perceptions, and it is impossible for humans to determine whether they are valid or fallacious, whereas testimony lacks these defects. Therefore, just as servants are rooted in the king, the other means of knowledge are rooted in testimony, but testimony itself is independent. They assist it only sometimes, according to their capacity, whereas testimony, being autonomous, is seen to operate even by overriding them. What is established by testimony cannot be contradicted by them, and for a substance untouchable by their powers, testimony alone is the most effective means of proof. For example, perception is of six types, being generated by the mind, intellect, and the five senses. Each of these is again of two types, determinate (savikalpaka) and indeterminate (nirvikalpaka), making twelve types. This is again of two types: expert and non-expert. In expert perception, there is freedom from human defects like contradiction and illusion, and testimony is also its basis. In non-expert perception, there is doubt. Its knowledge is fallible, as in 'Upon seeing an illusory head, one thinks, This is Devadatta's head."' But testimony is not like this, for example, 'There is snow in the Himalayas, there are jewels in the ocean.' Here, knowledge is rooted in testimony itself. For instance, when someone who has seen an illusory head before disbelieves even a real one out of illusion, even if he knows from a voice from the sky that 'This is real,' he cannot determine anything with certainty without serving the wise. This is the position of all logicians. The independence of testimony is seen in cases like 'You are the tenth person.' The word 'I am the tenth' immediately removes the illusion that obscures this valid knowledge, just by entering the ear, thus its independence is clear. Perception assists testimony according to its own power, as in 'Fire is the remedy for cold,' but not in 'You, my son, were in my womb in the city of Mathurā.' Testimony, however, can override perception, as with a mantra like 'There is no poison in you, though bitten by a snake.' What is established by testimony is not contradicted by perception, as in 'Calcined gold is smooth.' Testimony alone is the most effective means of proof, as in the case of planetary influences on a person's body. The position that 'what is established by everyone's perception is true' is defeated because it is impossible for everyone to gather in one place. The position that 'what is established by the perception of many' is also defeated, as it is seen that in some places or in man-made scriptures, there is erroneous knowledge of some object. Now, inference, which has five parts—thesis, reason, example, application, and conclusion—is also fallible. An example of a non-pervasive reason is on a mountain where a fire has just been extinguished by rain but smoke is still billowing for a long time, one infers, 'This mountain has fire,' or on a mountain that naturally steams during the rainy season. But testimony is not like this, as in 'Fire arises from a sunstone through a specific process.' Inference is rooted in testimony, as in 'O travelers afflicted by cold! Do not assume there is fire from this smoke; we saw it was just extinguished by rain.' or 'But fire is seen on that smoking mountain over there,' or 'This is just the appearance of smoke, there is no fire here, but there is over there.' If it is said that in the former case, the reason is fallacious because it is unproven, and therefore a valid inference is not fallible, we reply: because of the similarity in appearance, and because things like will-o'-the-wisps are seen on misty mountains, etc. Enough of this. Because things like smoke are not universal, and because smoke can exist from a past time even after the fire is out, and because knowledge of the presence or absence of fire comes only from the perception of fire or its absence, and knowledge of smoke comes from knowledge of fire, there would be mutual dependence. Thus, when such perception itself is fallible regarding valid knowledge, inference based on it is also fallible. The independence of testimony is seen in 'You are the tenth person.' It is assisted by inference according to its own power, as when those who have not seen the specific quality of a diamond infer that all earthy substances like stone can be cut by iron, but one who has heard of the quality of a diamond cannot infer that it can be cut by iron. Testimony can override inference, as in 'A limb heated by fire is soothed by the heat of fire,' or 'Ginger and other substances become sweet after being digested by the digestive fire.' What is established by testimony is not contradicted by inference, as in 'This one herb pacifies all three doshas.' For a subject untouchable by their powers, testimony alone is the most effective means of proof, as in planetary influences. Thus, with the two primary means of knowledge shown to be fallible, the others are naturally dependent, as they follow from testimony and these two. Now, to understand their nature, they are also presented. The speech of the gods and sages is 'ārṣa' (authoritative). The knowledge 'a gavaya is like a cow' is 'upamāna' (comparison). The fatness of someone who does not eat during the day implies that he eats at night; the assumption of this fact which cannot be otherwise explained is 'arthāpatti' (postulation). The senses do not grasp without contact; therefore, the means of knowing the absence of a pot is 'abhāva' (non-perception) in the form of its non-apprehension. The idea 'a hundred is possible in a thousand' is 'sambhava' (inclusion). Tradition passed down from an unknown speaker is 'aitihya' (tradition). Knowledge of ten pots from the raising of a finger is 'ceṣṭā' (gesture). Furthermore, because it is not different from that of animals, knowledge from perception and the like is not a means of knowing the ultimate truth. Their engagement and disengagement are seen based on sight, smell, etc., of desirable and undesirable things, but they attain no ultimate reality. It is seen that for very young children, all knowledge and activity come from the authoritative words of their mother, father, etc., and that those who are raised in isolation without it become dull and mute, and cannot function in society."
