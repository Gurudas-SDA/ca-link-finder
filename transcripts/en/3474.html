On this topic of the Veda's authority, Vācaspati says: 'It is not correct to say that the scripture (āmnāya), which depends on perception, is invalid or has a secondary meaning because it contradicts perception, the foremost means of knowledge. Because it is of non-human origin (apauruṣeya), the possibility of any defect is completely removed. As it is illuminating and its status as a valid means of knowledge is self-evident, it is independent in its own function of producing valid knowledge. Although it is independent in producing knowledge, it is dependent on perception for its origin. If it is said that its invalidity is due to its non-generation because it contradicts perception, this is not so, because it does not conflict with its producer. The knowledge from scripture does not destroy the conventional validity of perception, which would cause it not to arise due to the absence of a cause; rather, it destroys its absolute validity. And that absolute validity is not its producer, because it is seen that knowledge of the truth arises even from conventionally valid means of knowledge that are not absolutely real. For example, in a letter, qualities like being short or long, which belong to something else, are superimposed, yet they cause the understanding of the truth. People are not mistaken when they understand an elephant from the word 'nāga' or a tree from the word 'naga.'
