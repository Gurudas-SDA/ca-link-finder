The function of a word is threefold: primary (mukhyā), indicative (lakṣaṇā), and qualitative (gauṇī). The primary is twofold: conventional (rūḍhi) and etymological (yoga). Conventional applies by convention between a name and the named to an object that can be denoted by its form, species, or quality, as in 'Ḍittha' (a wooden elephant), 'a cow,' 'white.' The suggestive function (vyañjanā) is also considered a function. For example, when one says 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' the coolness, purity, etc., of the bank where it is situated are suggested. These then form a sentence and convey a specific meaning. 'A sentence is a collection of words possessing compatibility (yogyatā), expectancy (ākāṅkṣā), and proximity (āsatti)' [sā.da. 2.1]. Proximity is the uninterruptedness of the thought. Otherwise, the word 'Devadatta' uttered now would be connected with the word 'goes' uttered on another day. Here, although expectancy and compatibility are properties of the meaning related to the self, they are metaphorically attributed to the collection of words' [sā.da. 2.1 vṛttiḥ]. And that sentence is subordinate to a great sentence. A great sentence is a collection of sentences. Its meaning is determined by the introduction, conclusion, etc. Thus: 'Introduction and conclusion, repetition, originality, result, praise, and reasoning are the signs for determining the purport.' (1) The uniformity of the introduction and conclusion, (2) repetition, (3) originality (not known from other sources), (4) result or purpose, (5) praise (arthavāda), and (6) reasoning (upapatti)—these are the six signs of the purport. The meaning of a great sentence should also be understood by the general drift through positive and negative concomitance. Here, 'reasoning' does not mean being supported by dry logic, but only a certain possibility of it as stated in that scripture, because otherwise the scripture would be useless. Where there is a contradiction with another sentence, their relative strength should be considered. This can be (1) based on the scripture and (2) based on the statement. (1) An example of the former is 'In a conflict between śruti and smṛti, śruti is stronger.' (2) An example of the latter is 'Among direct statement (śruti), indication (liṅga), sentence (vākya), context (prakaraṇa), position (sthāna), and name (samākhyā), when they occur together, each succeeding one is weaker than the preceding one because of the remoteness of the meaning' [pū.mī.sū. 3.3.14]. And these are defined: 'Śruti is the word, liṅga is its capacity, vākya is the combination of words, prakaraṇa is the context which has expectancy, sthāna is the order, and samākhyā is the power of etymology.' And that contradiction, considering it to be based on metaphorical speech, etc., the meaning of the other sentence should be considered in accordance with the stronger sentence. This remoteness of logic has been explained only in the case of the inconceivability of the subject matter, as seen in statements like 'Those things which are inconceivable should not be subjected to logic' [ma.bhā. 6.6.11, and in Skanda Purāṇa].
