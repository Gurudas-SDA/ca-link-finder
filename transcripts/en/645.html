Because their practice is largely to obscure the beginningless, unbroken Veda, and because their conduct is to destroy the beginningless varṇāśrama system, and because they establish their own communities by corrupting the varṇas with their own food and customs, it is understood to be recent. It clearly follows that this scripture was established by someone just now. But is it not seen in the Veda as well, statements like 'stones float,' 'the earth spoke,' 'the waters spoke,' which seem to indicate unreliability (i.e., falsehood)? It is replied: This is a praise to enhance the power of the stones that are part of a specific ritual (in granting the fruit of the action). And since this is famously known in the construction of the bridge by Śrī Rāma, it is appropriate, so there is no fault. Similarly, in 'the earth spoke,' 'the waters spoke,' it should be known that the presiding deities of those elements are being referred to. Thus, in every way, the Veda is reliable. But because it is the word of the omniscient Lord, it is difficult for non-omniscient living beings to understand. Its meaning can be experienced everywhere only by those who have a special perception gained by His power, not by logicians. It is said in the Puruṣottama-tantra: 'Experience combined with the meaning of the scripture is considered the highest proof. Inference and other means do not independently attain the status of proof.' The same is the opinion of the author of the Brahma-sūtras, in statements like 'tarkāpratiṣṭhānāt' (because of the inconclusiveness of logic) [bra.sū. 2.1.11], and 'śrutes tu śabda-mūlatvāt' (but from śruti, because it is based on the word) [bra.sū. 2.1.27]. And the śruti says: 'This understanding is not to be attained by logic, O dearest; when taught by another, it leads to proper knowledge' [ka.u. 1.2.9], and 'Covered by mist and babbling' [ṛk saṁ 10.82.7]. The meaning of the śruti verse is that logicians are engaged in wrangling. Therefore, in the Varāha Purāṇa: 'Inference without scripture can be made everywhere. Therefore, it is not powerful enough to perceive without scripture.' The Advaita-vādins have said: 'A conclusion reached with great effort by skilled logicians is explained otherwise by even more expert ones' [vā.pu. 1.34]. And in the Advaita-śārīraka: 'It is not possible to gather all the logicians of the past, present, and future in one place and time, by which we could accept their opinion as correct knowledge. Since the Veda is eternal and the cause of the generation of knowledge, its meaning is fixed and established. The correctness of the knowledge generated by it cannot be denied by any logician of the past, present, or future' [bra.sū. 2.1.11, śā.bhā.]. When it is seen that something is taught through logic in the scripture, that is appropriate only there, because it is in the form of scripture, and because that logic is intended only to facilitate understanding. If it were that only that Vedic statement is authoritative which can be established by logic, then let there be only logic; what is the use of the Veda? The intention everywhere is that those who abandon the Veda are outsiders. Therefore, it is said in the Mahābhārata that their destination is to become jackals [ma.bhā. 12.180.47-49]. As for the acceptance of logic called 'manana' (reflection) in statements like 'śrotavyo mantavya' [bṛ.ā.u. 2.4.5], it is said in the Kūrma Purāṇa: 'The reasoning to determine what the intended meaning is without contradiction between the beginning and the end is called tarka. One should avoid dry logic.' Now, with the authority of all Vedic statements established, some say: the authority of the Veda lies only in matters of action (kārya), not in established realities (siddhe), because its potency (śakti) and purport (tātparya) are determined only in that context. The potency is understood as follows: when an elder says to a younger person, 'Bring the cow,' a child, observing him engaged in bringing the cow, understands that the meaning of this sentence is to bring the creature with a dewlap. Subsequently, in sentences like 'Graze the cow,' 'Bring the horse,' by the process of addition and subtraction, he determines that the meaning of the word 'go' (cow) is 'the creature with a dewlap,' and the meaning of the word 'ānayana' (bringing) is 'to fetch.' Thus, since from the very beginning the engagement is connected with action, the grasp of the word's potency is in that context. And so, the purport would also be there. To this it is replied: Why should there be no potency in established realities? Is it because the practical application that allows one to grasp the meaning is absent in established realities? Or is it because even there, it is connected to an action? The first is not correct, because the effect of a sentence like 'A son is born to you,' such as the happy expression on the face of the father who hears it, is observed. The second is also not correct, because there is no connection to an action in the birth of a son. Nor can an action like 'see him' be imagined here, because there is nothing to suggest such an imagination. If it is said that the impossibility of grasping the potency in something connected to an action at the outset is what suggests this, that is not so. This is because the grasp of potency in a sentence connected to an action is not established, because in the word 'kārya' (action) itself there is no connection to another action, thus there is a deviation. Also, if the grasp of meaning can be established simply by connection to something suitable, the specific qualification is useless. It cannot be said that in an action there is connection to another action, because that is not possible and would lead to an infinite regress. Nor is there a rule that the initial grasp of potency must be in connection to an action. A child's understanding is also seen in the statement of an established fact, like 'This is a cloth.' Therefore, when the potency in an established fact is established and there is no contradiction with the hearer's understanding, the speaker's purport will also be established there. Thus, the Upaniṣads and other texts that state established realities are also authoritative in their own right. It has been said: 'Therefore, even if mantras and arthavādas are for another purpose (i.e., karma), they have authority in their own right. If knowledge of an unobstructed, determined, and previously unknown object arises from a word by its own nature, why should it not be authoritative even without purport? In the case of praise and blame, it is a restatement or a secondary meaning. But the Upaniṣads, being subordinate to nothing else, and revealing the truth of the Self, which is free from all misfortune, is of the single taste of infinite bliss, and is otherwise unattainable, have authority in their own right, even in contradiction to other means of knowledge, by making those other means appear illusory' [Bhagavan-nāma-kaumudī 1.13]. Thus, with the authority of the Vedic word established in its own meaning, it is explained how it produces meaning. The letters, being perishable, cannot have the power to generate meaning. Therefore, it is thought that the final letter, along with the impressions generated by the preceding letters, conveys the meaning. And those impressions are inferred only from their effect, as they are not directly perceptible. Since the effect of the impressions, memory, occurs sequentially, there is no cognition of the whole. Thus, others, thinking that the final letter also cannot convey the meaning, say that the sphoṭa (an eternal, indivisible sound unit) is what conveys the meaning. 'And that sphoṭa, because a single cognition is not possible from multiple letters, flashes into the mind at once as the object of a single cognition, in the mind where the seed of impression has been sown by the cognition of each letter and has been brought to maturity by the cognition of the final letter' [bra.sū. 1.3.28, śā.bhā.]. Therefore, the Veda is eternal because it is of the nature of sphoṭa, as it is recognized upon each utterance [bra.sū. 1.3.28, śā.bhā.]. The Vedāntins, however, following the maxim of the revered Upavarṣa, 'The letters themselves are the word' [Jaimini's Dvādaśa-lakṣaṇī 1.5.5, Śabara's commentary], and because it is universally recognized as one, as in 'The word 'go' has been uttered twice,' not 'two 'go' words,' they accept the eternality of the words which are composed of letters. And those letters, like a line of ants, being connected to a specific meaning supported by sequence, etc., and appearing in the mind which perceives all the letters after the perception of each one in practical usage, will invariably convey that meaning. Therefore, the theory of the letter-proponents is simpler. For the sphoṭa-proponents, there is the abandonment of the seen and the assumption of the unseen. And the theory that these letters, being perceived sequentially, manifest the sphoṭa, and that sphoṭa manifests the meaning, is a more cumbersome assumption. Thus, the eternality and meaning-conveying capacity of the Vedic words, which are in the form of letters, is accepted. The function of a word is threefold: primary (mukhyā), indicative (lakṣaṇā), and qualitative (gauṇī). The primary is twofold: conventional (rūḍhi) and etymological (yoga). Conventional applies by convention between a name and the named to an object that can be denoted by its form, species, or quality, as in 'Ḍittha' (a wooden elephant), 'a cow,' 'white.' Indicative relates to something connected with the meaning conveyed by that same convention, as in 'A village on the Gaṅgā.' This is of three types: that which does not abandon its own meaning (ajahat-svārthā), that which abandons its own meaning (jahat-svārthā), and that which both abandons and does not abandon its own meaning (jahad-ajahat-svārthā). Examples are, respectively, 'The white is running,' 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' 'This is that Devadatta.' The last type is not accepted by Śrī Rāmānuja and others; this should be researched in their works. In the example 'This is that Devadatta,' the word 'that' refers to what was experienced at that time, and 'this' refers to what is being experienced now. There is no contradiction in their combination, so how can there be indication? This is a summary. Qualitative (gauṇī) applies to something similar that possesses the qualities indicated by the primary meaning, as in 'Devadatta is a lion.' As they say: 'The function of indication is accepted when it is inseparable from the primary meaning. The qualitative function is desired when it is based on the connection with the indicated qualities' [ta.vā. 1.4.22]. Here, 'lakṣaṇā' depends on either 'rūḍhi' or a purpose. An example of the former is 'A Kaliṅga is brave.' An example of the latter is 'A village on the Gaṅgā.' Here, the purpose is to convey the coolness and purity of the bank. In the qualitative function, only a purpose is required. For example, in 'A Vāhīka is a bull,' the purpose is to convey extreme ignorance, etc. 'yoga' is the combination of the meanings of the base and the suffix of the words conveyed by these three functions, as in 'paṅkajam' (lotus), 'aupagavaḥ' (descendant of Upagu), 'pācakaḥ' (cook). The suggestive function (vyañjanā) is also considered a function. For example, when one says 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' the coolness, purity, etc., of the bank where it is situated are suggested. It is said: 'By the principle that word, intellect, and action do not have a separate function after their own is complete, when the three functions of primary meaning, indication, and purport have conveyed their respective meanings and ceased, that by which another meaning is conveyed is called suggestion (vyañjanā). It is the power of the word, its meaning, the base, suffix, etc., and is referred to by terms like suggestion, implication, resonance, conveyance, and intention' [sā.da. 2.19, vṛtti]. Now, these functions arise to convey their respective meanings only in words that have formed into sentences. A word becomes a word by being joined with a case ending. These then form a sentence and convey a specific meaning. 'A sentence is a collection of words possessing compatibility (yogyatā), expectancy (ākāṅkṣā), and proximity (āsatti)' [sā.da. 2.1]. 'Compatibility is the absence of contradiction in the mutual connection of the meanings of the words. Otherwise, 'He sprinkles with fire' would also be a sentence.' In statements like 'Prajāpati tore out his own omentum' [tai.sa. 2.5.1], however, because such beings have inconceivable power, there is compatibility. 'Expectancy is the absence of the completion of the thought, in the form of the hearer's inquiry. Otherwise, 'A cow, a horse,' 'a man, an elephant' would also be sentences. Proximity is the uninterruptedness of the thought. Otherwise, the word 'Devadatta' uttered now would be connected with the word 'goes' uttered on another day. Here, although expectancy and compatibility are properties of the meaning related to the self, they are metaphorically attributed to the collection of words' [sā.da. 2.1 vṛttiḥ]. And that sentence is subordinate to a great sentence. A great sentence is a collection of sentences. Its meaning is determined by the introduction, conclusion, etc. Thus: 'Introduction and conclusion, repetition, originality, result, praise, and reasoning are the signs for determining the purport.' (1) The uniformity of the introduction and conclusion, (2) repetition, (3) originality (not known from other sources), (4) result or purpose, (5) praise (arthavāda), and (6) reasoning (upapatti)—these are the six signs of the purport. The meaning of a great sentence should also be understood by the general drift through positive and negative concomitance. Here, 'reasoning' does not mean being supported by dry logic, but only a certain possibility of it as stated in that scripture, because otherwise the scripture would be useless. Where there is a contradiction with another sentence, their relative strength should be considered. This can be (1) based on the scripture and (2) based on the statement. (1) An example of the former is 'In a conflict between śruti and smṛti, śruti is stronger.' (2) An example of the latter is 'Among direct statement (śruti), indication (liṅga), sentence (vākya), context (prakaraṇa), position (sthāna), and name (samākhyā), when they occur together, each succeeding one is weaker than the preceding one because of the remoteness of the meaning' [pū.mī.sū. 3.3.14]. And these are defined: 'Śruti is the word, liṅga is its capacity, vākya is the combination of words, prakaraṇa is the context which has expectancy, sthāna is the order, and samākhyā is the power of etymology.' And that contradiction, considering it to be based on metaphorical speech, etc., the meaning of the other sentence should be considered in accordance with the stronger sentence. This remoteness of logic has been explained only in the case of the inconceivability of the subject matter, as seen in statements like 'Those things which are inconceivable should not be subjected to logic' [ma.bhā. 6.6.11, and in Skanda Purāṇa]. But in conceivable matters, logic may also find a place; let it, we have no insistence on that. In every way, the Veda alone is the valid means of knowledge.
