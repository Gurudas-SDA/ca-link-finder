The potency is understood as follows: when an elder says to a younger person, 'Bring the cow,' a child, observing him engaged in bringing the cow, understands that the meaning of this sentence is to bring the creature with a dewlap. Subsequently, in sentences like 'Graze the cow,' 'Bring the horse,' by the process of addition and subtraction, he determines that the meaning of the word 'go' (cow) is 'the creature with a dewlap,' and the meaning of the word 'ānayana' (bringing) is 'to fetch.' Thus, since from the very beginning the engagement is connected with action, the grasp of the word's potency is in that context. And so, the purport would also be there. Or is it because even there, it is connected to an action? The second is also not correct, because there is no connection to an action in the birth of a son. Nor can an action like 'see him' be imagined here, because there is nothing to suggest such an imagination. If it is said that the impossibility of grasping the potency in something connected to an action at the outset is what suggests this, that is not so. This is because the grasp of potency in a sentence connected to an action is not established, because in the word 'kārya' (action) itself there is no connection to another action, thus there is a deviation. Also, if the grasp of meaning can be established simply by connection to something suitable, the specific qualification is useless. It cannot be said that in an action there is connection to another action, because that is not possible and would lead to an infinite regress. Nor is there a rule that the initial grasp of potency must be in connection to an action. A child's understanding is also seen in the statement of an established fact, like 'This is a cloth.' Therefore, when the potency in an established fact is established and there is no contradiction with the hearer's understanding, the speaker's purport will also be established there. Thus, the Upaniṣads and other texts that state established realities are also authoritative in their own right. If knowledge of an unobstructed, determined, and previously unknown object arises from a word by its own nature, why should it not be authoritative even without purport? In the case of praise and blame, it is a restatement or a secondary meaning. Thus, with the authority of the Vedic word established in its own meaning, it is explained how it produces meaning. The letters, being perishable, cannot have the power to generate meaning. Thus, others, thinking that the final letter also cannot convey the meaning, say that the sphoṭa (an eternal, indivisible sound unit) is what conveys the meaning. Conventional applies by convention between a name and the named to an object that can be denoted by its form, species, or quality, as in 'Ḍittha' (a wooden elephant), 'a cow,' 'white.' Indicative relates to something connected with the meaning conveyed by that same convention, as in 'A village on the Gaṅgā.' The suggestive function (vyañjanā) is also considered a function. For example, when one says 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' the coolness, purity, etc., of the bank where it is situated are suggested. 'Expectancy is the absence of the completion of the thought, in the form of the hearer's inquiry. Otherwise, 'A cow, a horse,' 'a man, an elephant' would also be sentences. Proximity is the uninterruptedness of the thought. Otherwise, the word 'Devadatta' uttered now would be connected with the word 'goes' uttered on another day. Here, although expectancy and compatibility are properties of the meaning related to the self, they are metaphorically attributed to the collection of words' [sā.da. 2.1 vṛttiḥ]. And that sentence is subordinate to a great sentence. A great sentence is a collection of sentences. Its meaning is determined by the introduction, conclusion, etc. Thus: 'Introduction and conclusion, repetition, originality, result, praise, and reasoning are the signs for determining the purport.' (1) The uniformity of the introduction and conclusion, (2) repetition, (3) originality (not known from other sources), (4) result or purpose, (5) praise (arthavāda), and (6) reasoning (upapatti)—these are the six signs of the purport. The meaning of a great sentence should also be understood by the general drift through positive and negative concomitance. Here, 'reasoning' does not mean being supported by dry logic, but only a certain possibility of it as stated in that scripture, because otherwise the scripture would be useless. Where there is a contradiction with another sentence, their relative strength should be considered. This can be (1) based on the scripture and (2) based on the statement. (1) An example of the former is 'In a conflict between śruti and smṛti, śruti is stronger.' (2) An example of the latter is 'Among direct statement (śruti), indication (liṅga), sentence (vākya), context (prakaraṇa), position (sthāna), and name (samākhyā), when they occur together, each succeeding one is weaker than the preceding one because of the remoteness of the meaning' [pū.mī.sū. 3.3.14]. And these are defined: 'Śruti is the word, liṅga is its capacity, vākya is the combination of words, prakaraṇa is the context which has expectancy, sthāna is the order, and samākhyā is the power of etymology.' And that contradiction, considering it to be based on metaphorical speech, etc., the meaning of the other sentence should be considered in accordance with the stronger sentence. This remoteness of logic has been explained only in the case of the inconceivability of the subject matter, as seen in statements like 'Those things which are inconceivable should not be subjected to logic' [ma.bhā. 6.6.11, and in Skanda Purāṇa]. But in conceivable matters, logic may also find a place; let it, we have no insistence on that. In every way, the Veda alone is the valid means of knowledge. It is said in Śaṅkara's Śārīraka: 'A proponent of Brahman determines the nature of the cause, etc., on the strength of scripture. It is not necessary that he should accept everything as it is seen (by inference)' [bra.sū. 2.2.38]. Thus, the Veda is non-material sound, and its supreme object will, by its non-material nature, be inconceivable. In searching for it, that which is established above all others by its introduction and other signs is to be worshiped. ||11||
