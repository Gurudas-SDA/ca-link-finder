This is because the grasp of potency in a sentence connected to an action is not established, because in the word 'kārya' (action) itself there is no connection to another action, thus there is a deviation. Also, if the grasp of meaning can be established simply by connection to something suitable, the specific qualification is useless. It cannot be said that in an action there is connection to another action, because that is not possible and would lead to an infinite regress. Therefore, when the potency in an established fact is established and there is no contradiction with the hearer's understanding, the speaker's purport will also be established there. The function of a word is threefold: primary (mukhyā), indicative (lakṣaṇā), and qualitative (gauṇī). The primary is twofold: conventional (rūḍhi) and etymological (yoga). Conventional applies by convention between a name and the named to an object that can be denoted by its form, species, or quality, as in 'Ḍittha' (a wooden elephant), 'a cow,' 'white.' Indicative relates to something connected with the meaning conveyed by that same convention, as in 'A village on the Gaṅgā.' This is of three types: that which does not abandon its own meaning (ajahat-svārthā), that which abandons its own meaning (jahat-svārthā), and that which both abandons and does not abandon its own meaning (jahad-ajahat-svārthā). Examples are, respectively, 'The white is running,' 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' 'This is that Devadatta.' The last type is not accepted by Śrī Rāmānuja and others; this should be researched in their works. In the example 'This is that Devadatta,' the word 'that' refers to what was experienced at that time, and 'this' refers to what is being experienced now. There is no contradiction in their combination, so how can there be indication? This is a summary. Qualitative (gauṇī) applies to something similar that possesses the qualities indicated by the primary meaning, as in 'Devadatta is a lion.' As they say: 'The function of indication is accepted when it is inseparable from the primary meaning. An example of the former is 'A Kaliṅga is brave.' An example of the latter is 'A village on the Gaṅgā.' Here, the purpose is to convey the coolness and purity of the bank. In the qualitative function, only a purpose is required. For example, in 'A Vāhīka is a bull,' the purpose is to convey extreme ignorance, etc. 'yoga' is the combination of the meanings of the base and the suffix of the words conveyed by these three functions, as in 'paṅkajam' (lotus), 'aupagavaḥ' (descendant of Upagu), 'pācakaḥ' (cook). The suggestive function (vyañjanā) is also considered a function. For example, when one says 'A village on the Gaṅgā,' the coolness, purity, etc., of the bank where it is situated are suggested. It is said: 'By the principle that word, intellect, and action do not have a separate function after their own is complete, when the three functions of primary meaning, indication, and purport have conveyed their respective meanings and ceased, that by which another meaning is conveyed is called suggestion (vyañjanā). It is the power of the word, its meaning, the base, suffix, etc., and is referred to by terms like suggestion, implication, resonance, conveyance, and intention' [sā.da. 2.19, vṛtti]. Now, these functions arise to convey their respective meanings only in words that have formed into sentences. A word becomes a word by being joined with a case ending. These then form a sentence and convey a specific meaning. 'A sentence is a collection of words possessing compatibility (yogyatā), expectancy (ākāṅkṣā), and proximity (āsatti)' [sā.da. 2.1]. 'Compatibility is the absence of contradiction in the mutual connection of the meanings of the words. Otherwise, 'He sprinkles with fire' would also be a sentence.' In statements like 'Prajāpati tore out his own omentum' [tai.sa. 2.5.1], however, because such beings have inconceivable power, there is compatibility. 'Expectancy is the absence of the completion of the thought, in the form of the hearer's inquiry. Otherwise, 'A cow, a horse,' 'a man, an elephant' would also be sentences. Proximity is the uninterruptedness of the thought. Otherwise, the word 'Devadatta' uttered now would be connected with the word 'goes' uttered on another day. Here, although expectancy and compatibility are properties of the meaning related to the self, they are metaphorically attributed to the collection of words' [sā.da. 2.1 vṛttiḥ]. And that sentence is subordinate to a great sentence. A great sentence is a collection of sentences. Its meaning is determined by the introduction, conclusion, etc. Thus: 'Introduction and conclusion, repetition, originality, result, praise, and reasoning are the signs for determining the purport.' (1) The uniformity of the introduction and conclusion, (2) repetition, (3) originality (not known from other sources), (4) result or purpose, (5) praise (arthavāda), and (6) reasoning (upapatti)—these are the six signs of the purport. The meaning of a great sentence should also be understood by the general drift through positive and negative concomitance. Here, 'reasoning' does not mean being supported by dry logic, but only a certain possibility of it as stated in that scripture, because otherwise the scripture would be useless. Where there is a contradiction with another sentence, their relative strength should be considered. This can be (1) based on the scripture and (2) based on the statement. (1) An example of the former is 'In a conflict between śruti and smṛti, śruti is stronger.' (2) An example of the latter is 'Among direct statement (śruti), indication (liṅga), sentence (vākya), context (prakaraṇa), position (sthāna), and name (samākhyā), when they occur together, each succeeding one is weaker than the preceding one because of the remoteness of the meaning' [pū.mī.sū. 3.3.14]. And these are defined: 'Śruti is the word, liṅga is its capacity, vākya is the combination of words, prakaraṇa is the context which has expectancy, sthāna is the order, and samākhyā is the power of etymology.' And that contradiction, considering it to be based on metaphorical speech, etc., the meaning of the other sentence should be considered in accordance with the stronger sentence. This remoteness of logic has been explained only in the case of the inconceivability of the subject matter, as seen in statements like 'Those things which are inconceivable should not be subjected to logic' [ma.bhā. 6.6.11, and in Skanda Purāṇa]. But in conceivable matters, logic may also find a place; let it, we have no insistence on that. In every way, the Veda alone is the valid means of knowledge. It is said in Śaṅkara's Śārīraka: 'A proponent of Brahman determines the nature of the cause, etc., on the strength of scripture. It is not necessary that he should accept everything as it is seen (by inference)' [bra.sū. 2.2.38]. Thus, the Veda is non-material sound, and its supreme object will, by its non-material nature, be inconceivable. In searching for it, that which is established above all others by its introduction and other signs is to be worshiped. ||11||
